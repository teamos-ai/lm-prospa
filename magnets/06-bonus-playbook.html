The Bonus Playbook

A bonus lands. You have about two weeks before it quietly disappears. Pick your play first.

Why a rule beats a decision

A bonus arrives alongside a reason to celebrate, a renovation quote and a car that is three years old. Every one of those is reasonable. A rule set in a calm month is not competing with any of them.

The same goes for a vesting parcel, a distribution, or the month a loan finally clears. Irregular income is where the largest single decisions get made fastest, and with the least thought.

What actually lands

A bonus is announced in gross and spent in net. Start from the smaller number — it is the only one you can place.

$

A simplification: it applies one marginal rate to the whole amount and ignores any pre-tax arrangement, offsets, HELP repayments or the possibility that the bonus pushes you into a higher bracket. It is here to make the gap between gross and net visible, not to calculate your tax.

The five plays

Each play sends the money somewhere different, and each one costs you something. Give every play a share of the net until the five of them add to 100%. The shares already filled in are an illustrative split — a starting point to argue with, not a recommendation.

Where the net goes

Reachable, or locked

The same split read a different way: how much of it you could actually get to if you needed it.

Money in the home loan is reachable only through a redraw facility or an offset account you actually have. Money in super is subject to the annual contribution caps and locked until preservation age.

The decision made in the moment is almost never the one you would have chosen in advance.

The rule, in my own words Playbook 06 · Decided in advance

Four ways this comes apart

Every one of these is something sensible people do.

Letting it sit until you decide

Money parked in a transaction account pending a decision is money being spent in instalments. The decision is usually made by attrition rather than by choice.

Treating the headline figure as the budget

Commitments get made against the announced number, and the net arrives months later roughly half the size. The shortfall is then funded by the surplus that was supposed to be invested.

Topping up super without checking the cap

Concessional contributions include what your employer already pays. Exceeding the cap has tax consequences, and the carry-forward rules that might have helped depend on your total super balance. Check before, not after.

Giving every dollar to the sensible plays

A rule with no enjoyment in it does not survive its second year. A deliberate small share for something you actually want is what makes the other 90% automatic.
