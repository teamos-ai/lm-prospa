The Estate Checklist

The one job where the cost of skipping it is paid entirely by other people.

Four documents decide where your money goes. Your will is only one of them.

Why this sheet exists

What decides where each thing goes

A different document governs each kind of asset. Three of them sit outside your will entirely, and they do not have to agree with it.

  • The asset Your superannuation
    Decided by Your beneficiary nomination

    Superannuation is held in trust and is not automatically an estate asset. Where it goes is usually decided by your beneficiary nomination, or by the trustee if there is no valid one.

    Commonly assumed “My will covers my superannuation.”

  • The asset A home held jointly
    Decided by The title

    Property held as joint tenants typically passes directly to the surviving joint owner by survivorship, outside the will. Property held as tenants in common does pass through the estate. The difference is on the title.

    Commonly assumed “My will covers the house.”

  • The asset Anything inside a trust or company
    Decided by The deed

    A trust does not die with you. Control passes under the deed, not under the will — so the question to ask is who the deed hands control to next.

    Commonly assumed “It forms part of my estate.”

  • The asset What is left in your own name
    Decided by Your will

    This is the part your will actually governs — and, of the four, the only part it governs at all. Everything above is decided somewhere else.

The four kinds of super nomination

Not all of them bind the trustee, and not all funds offer all of them. Find out which one you actually have.

  • Non-binding

    A preference only

    The trustee considers your wishes but decides. The most common nomination, and the least certain.

  • Binding lapsing

    Binds, while valid

    The trustee must follow it — for as long as it remains in force.

    This is the one that expires. A lapsing nomination typically expires after three years, and expiry is silent: nothing announces it, and the decision reverts to trustee discretion.

  • Binding non-lapsing

    Binds, while valid

    Does not expire by time, but must still be valid, and is only offered by some funds. Review it after any life change.

  • Reversionary pension

    Binds — the income stream continues

    An existing pension continues to a nominated person rather than paying a lump sum. It interacts with that person's transfer balance cap.

Who can be nominated. Generally a spouse, a child, a financial dependant, someone in an interdependency relationship, or your legal personal representative — your estate. Nominating anyone outside those categories usually makes the nomination invalid, which returns the decision to the trustee.

Who receives it changes what is paid

Broadly, for a lump sum death benefit from a taxed source. Super is often assumed to reach adult children tax-free; that holds only where they are tax dependants, and financially independent adult children usually are not.

Before you start

You will never see the benefit of this work. The people who will are the ones who have to find the documents, ring the fund and make decisions in a week they did not choose.

So treat it as four short sittings rather than one long one, and tick only what is genuinely current. An unticked box is not a failure — it is the agenda for one conversation.

The one test worth applying

Take your largest three assets. For each, name the document that decides where it goes — the will, a nomination, the title, or a deed. If you cannot name it for all three, that is the gap, and it is usually closed in a single conversation between your adviser and your estate lawyer.

General information only, and not legal advice. Estate planning involves succession law, which differs between Australian states and territories, as well as tax and superannuation law. The descriptions here are general, simplified and subject to exceptions — the treatment of any particular asset depends on how it is held, the terms of the relevant trust deed or policy, and your personal circumstances. Wills, powers of attorney, binding nominations and trust succession should be prepared or reviewed by a qualified estate lawyer, with tax and superannuation input where relevant.
