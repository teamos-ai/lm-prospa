The Estate & Beneficiary Checklist

This is the one area where the cost of leaving it is paid entirely by other people — and where the documents most often contradict each other without anyone noticing. Your will does not control everything you own.

Three things people reasonably assume

And what is generally the case instead.

The assumption

"My will covers my superannuation."

Generally

Superannuation is held in trust and is not automatically an estate asset. Where it goes is usually decided by your beneficiary nomination, or by the trustee if there is no valid one — not by your will.

The assumption

"My will covers the house."

Generally

Property held as joint tenants typically passes directly to the surviving joint owner by survivorship, outside the will. Property held as tenants in common does pass through the estate. The difference is on the title.

The assumption

"My children will receive my super tax-free."

Generally

Only if they are tax dependants. Financially independent adult children usually are not, and tax can apply to the taxable component of a lump sum death benefit paid to them.

The four kinds of super nomination

Not all of them bind the trustee, and not all funds offer all of them. Check which one you actually have.

Type Binds the trustee? What to watch
Non-binding No — a preference only The trustee considers your wishes but decides. The most common nomination, and the least certain.
Binding lapsing Yes, while valid Typically expires after three years. An expired nomination reverts to trustee discretion, and expiry is silent.
Binding non-lapsing Yes, while valid Does not expire by time, but must still be valid and is only offered by some funds. Review it after any life change.
Reversionary pension Yes — the income stream continues An existing pension continues to a nominated person rather than paying a lump sum. Interacts with their transfer balance cap.

Who can be nominated. Generally a spouse, a child, a financial dependant, someone in an interdependency relationship, or your legal personal representative — your estate. Nominating anyone outside those categories usually makes the nomination invalid, which returns the decision to the trustee.

Who receives it changes what is paid

Broadly, for a lump sum death benefit from a taxed source.

Recipient Tax-free component Taxable component (taxed element)
Tax dependantSpouse, child under 18, financial dependant, interdependency Nil Nil
Non-tax dependantMost commonly a financially independent adult child Nil Up to 15%plus Medicare levy where paid directly to an individual

An untaxed element — which can arise in some schemes and where life cover forms part of the benefit — is taxed at a higher rate again. Where a benefit is paid through the estate rather than directly, the Medicare levy generally does not apply, though the underlying tax treatment still follows who ultimately benefits. This is a simplified summary of a genuinely technical area.

The one test worth applying

Take your largest three assets. For each, name the document that decides where it goes — the will, a nomination, the title, or a deed. If you cannot name it for all three, that is the gap, and it is usually resolved in a single conversation between your adviser and your estate lawyer.

General information only, and not legal advice. Estate planning involves succession law, which differs between Australian states and territories, as well as tax and superannuation law. The descriptions here are general, simplified and subject to exceptions — the treatment of any particular asset depends on how it is held, the terms of the relevant trust deed or policy, and your personal circumstances. Wills, powers of attorney, binding nominations and trust succession should be prepared or reviewed by a qualified estate lawyer, with tax and superannuation input where relevant.
